# Sources and methodology

Blue Language is original course material. No commercial textbook passages, proprietary exercises, answer keys, dictionary definitions, or copyrighted word-list explanations were copied. External references were used to calibrate CEFR performance, grammar coverage, vocabulary priority, phraseology, register, and task design.

## 1. Primary CEFR framework
- **Council of Europe — Common European Framework of Reference for Languages: Learning, Teaching, Assessment — Companion Volume (2020).** The course uses the CEFR as an action-oriented performance framework: progression is defined by what learners can understand, express, mediate, and accomplish, not by a mechanical grammar checklist.
- The 2020 Companion Volume is particularly important for advanced levels because it expands descriptors for **mediation, online interaction, plurilingual/pluricultural competence, and related discourse activities**.

## 2. Cambridge calibration and exam-facing performance
- **Cambridge English / English Profile.** CEFR-linked learner-language research and grammar/vocabulary profiling were used as calibration references rather than a rigid one-form-per-level syllabus.
- **Cambridge C1 Advanced** performance descriptions were used to check that C1 tasks require confident academic/professional communication, extended argument, seminar/meeting participation, and high fluency.
- **Cambridge C2 Proficiency** performance descriptions were used to check that C2 work requires sophisticated, flexible communication and control of complex or delicate issues rather than merely rarer vocabulary.

## 3. Vocabulary, phraseology and academic language
- **Oxford Learner’s Dictionaries — Oxford 3000 and Oxford 5000.** Frequency, learner relevance, corpus evidence, and CEFR alignment informed lexical prioritization. Oxford describes the Oxford 5000 as adding 2,000 advanced words aligned mainly to B2–C1; Blue Language therefore does **not** treat it as a complete C2 list.
- **Oxford Phrase List and OPAL (Oxford Phrasal Academic Lexicon).** These informed the emphasis on phraseology, academic/professional combinations, and spoken/written academic language.
- For C2, lexical design places greater weight on **collocation, semantic nuance, evidential language, pragmatic meaning, register, source-sensitive reporting, reformulation, and discourse phraseology**, because CEFR C2 proficiency cannot be reduced to a fixed headword list.

## 4. Grammar and usage reference orientation
The following major reference works informed standard distinctions and terminology. Blue Language wording and exercises remain original.
- Martin Hewings — *Advanced Grammar in Use* (Cambridge University Press).
- Raymond Murphy — *English Grammar in Use* (Cambridge University Press), especially for lower/intermediate contrast checking.
- Michael Swan — *Practical English Usage* (Oxford University Press).
- Rodney Huddleston & Geoffrey K. Pullum — *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* (Cambridge University Press), for comprehensive grammatical reference.
- Ronald Carter & Michael McCarthy — *Cambridge Grammar of English* (Cambridge University Press), particularly useful for spoken/written usage orientation.
- Douglas Biber, Stig Johansson, Geoffrey Leech, Susan Conrad & Edward Finegan — *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* (Pearson/Longman), for register-sensitive corpus-based grammar orientation.

## 5. Vocabulary-learning and corpus-informed design principles
- Vocabulary selection favors **frequency, usefulness, dispersion across contexts, phraseological behavior, and retrieval value**, not lexical rarity for its own sake.
- Advanced items are taught as words **plus their typical frames, collocations, stance, and register** whenever relevant.
- The course avoids the false progression model “higher CEFR = longer words.” B2–C2 progression increasingly depends on discourse management, pragmatic inference, precision, flexibility, and audience adaptation.

## 6. Pedagogical architecture
1. **Meaning before form.** Grammar targets are attached to a communicative purpose: time reference, stance, focus, cause, contrast, source status, hypothetical reasoning, politeness, or information packaging.
2. **Notice → contrast → retrieve → produce.** Units move from contextual input and explanation into controlled retrieval and then productive writing/speaking.
3. **Spiral progression.** Later levels reuse earlier grammar inside richer discourse without repeating vocabulary-bank headwords across levels.
4. **Retrieval with explanation.** Practice and tests include answer explanations so learners can correct the underlying rule rather than memorize a letter choice.
5. **Register and discourse at advanced levels.** C1–C2 grammar is treated as a choice among alternatives according to genre, audience, information focus, stance, and rhetorical force.
6. **Mediation and reformulation.** Advanced tasks include converting specialist or dense information for another audience while preserving evidence, uncertainty, and logical relations.
7. **Originality.** Reference works guide calibration; all Blue Language lesson passages, model answers, test items, examples, and Arabic glosses are independently authored.

## 7. Internal quality-control rules
- Exact JSON schema and content order are machine checked.
- Reading word counts are checked against the prompt’s CEFR bands.
- Grammar lessons are checked at 700–1100 words with the required heading order.
- Vocabulary examples are checked at 12–20 words.
- Vocabulary headwords/phrases are checked for exact duplicates across all 12 levels.
- Vocabulary example sentences and grammar example lines receive an exact-duplicate audit across the full package.
- Practice/test question structure, MCQ option uniqueness, correct-answer availability, listening TTS fields, image filenames, and image-list references are validated automatically.
